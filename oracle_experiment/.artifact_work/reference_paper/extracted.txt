       Abstract. University students routinely confront fragmented information ecosys-
       tems: unstructured documents (e.g., regulations, policies) coexist with structured
       databases (e.g., individual records), yet most conversational AI systems can han-
        dle only one modality and thus fail on complex, hybrid queries that re-quire cross-
       source synthesis. To close this gap, we propose Academic Query Orchestration,
       a novel AI-agent framework whose core “brain” is guided by a carefully engi-
       neered Super-Structured Prompt to reason, plan, and orchestrate a Specialized
       Tool Suite comprising a RAG-based Knowledge Retriever and a safety-hardened

      decompose hybrid questions and fuse heterogeneous evidence into a coherent
       answer, reinforced by a feedback loop for self-repair. For rigorous validation, we
       conduct a comprehensive evaluation on established public benchmarks. Our ap-
       proach achieves competitive performance on single-modality specialized tasks
        (including complex Text-to-SQL and knowledge-intensive QA) and—cru-
        cially—substantially outperforms strong baselines on a challenging benchmark
        purpose-built for hybrid, multi-source reasoning.

1    Introduction

  The ongoing digital transformation in higher education has produced an information
ecosystem  that  is both complex and fragmented  for  students. Core academic
knowledge—such as institutional policies, course catalogs, and student handbooks—
often resides in unstructured formats dispersed across numerous documents and web-
sites, whereas personal learning data (e.g., grades, timetables, and financial status) is
secured within structured databases. This fragmentation renders student information
seeking discontinuous, time-consuming, and error-prone, thereby degrading the student
===== PAGE 2 =====

experience and increasing operational burden on administrative units [1]. In response,
conversational AI systems, propelled by recent advances in Large Language Models
(LLMs), have emerged as a promising paradigm for delivering a unified, real-time ac-
cess point to institutional and personal information, with substantial potential to im-
prove accessibility, consistency, and the quality of student support [2].
  However, directly deploying general-purpose LLMs in this specialized context en-
tails substantial challenges. LLMs are prone to factual inaccuracies—so-called “hallu-
cinations”—and their parametric knowledge quickly becomes outdated, rendering them
unreliable for queries that depend on institution-specific and rapidly evolving policies
[3]. To mitigate these limitations, Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) has been
recognized as an effective approach to anchor LLM outputs to authoritative external
knowledge bases, thereby improving factual accuracy and enabling source verification
[4]. In parallel, for accessing structured data, the Text-to-SQL task has emerged as a
central research focus that translates natural-language questions into executable data-
base queries [5]; notably, the advent of LLMs has substantially reshaped methodologi-
cal paradigms for this problem. Despite the complementary strengths of RAG (for un-
structured corpora) and Text-to-SQL (for relational databases), most existing work
treats them as independent lines of inquiry. Consequently, a significant research gap
remains in addressing hybrid, compositionally complex queries that require rigorous
synthesis,  linkage,  and  cross-validation  of  evidence  across  both  unstructured
knowledge sources and structured database systems.
  To bridge this gap, we propose Academic Query Orchestration (AQO), a novel AI-
agent framework tailored to higher-education settings. AQO departs from monolithic
designs by instantiating the agent as an intelligent orchestrator that leverages a toolbox
of specialized modules. The toolbox comprises two principal capabilities: (i) a RAG
module for faithful, citation-aware answers grounded in the university’s unstructured
knowledge base, and (ii) a safe Text-to-SQL module for executing least-privilege, au-
ditable queries over students’ structured records. The core innovation lies in the agent’s
reasoning and action planning: inspired by ReAct-style reasoning-and-acting loops, the
orchestrator can decompose user requests, select and sequence tools (RAG, Text-to-
SQL, or a composed pipeline of both), and synthesize retrieved evidence into a single,
coherent response with source attribution [6]. This orchestration enables principled
handling of hybrid, compositionally complex queries while maintaining factual ground-
ing, policy compliance, and end-to-end answer verifiability.

2    Related work

  The application of conversational AI to student services in higher education has long
been an active research area. Early efforts centered on rule-based chatbots and FAQ-
style question-answering systems which, while useful within narrow scopes, exhibited
conversational rigidity and incurred substantial maintenance overhead as content
evolved [2]. The recent emergence of LLMs marks a significant inflection point, ena-
bling more sophisticated virtual assistants with fluent natural-language interaction.
Nevertheless, as discussed by Kasneci et  al. (2023)  [7], these opportunities are

accompanied by notable challenges, particularly ensuring factual reliability and do-
main-specific correctness. A persistent limitation of many existing systems is their ori-
entation toward delivering general, static information; they typically lack the capability
to access and interact—securely, in real time—with structured, student-specific data
(e.g., grades, timetables, enrollment status) that reside within institutional databases.
  To address the issues of hallucination and stale parametric knowledge LLMs, Re-
trieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) has been introduced as a breakthrough approach
[4]. The core principle of RAG is to anchor generation to an authoritative external
knowledge store: relevant passages are retrieved from a domain corpus and supplied as
additional context to the LLM, thereby substantially improving factual accuracy and
enabling source citation—a critical requirement in academic settings. The effectiveness
of this paradigm has spurred a wave of research and applications across knowledge-
intensive domains. As summarized in a recent survey by [8], numerous advanced RAG
techniques have been developed, focusing on optimizing key components such as re-
trieval, re-ranking, and context integration strategies. Nevertheless, despite its strengths
for unstructured knowledge, RAG remains fundamentally constrained to text corpora.
It is not designed to interact directly with structured data or to answer queries requiring
dynamic, real-time information from relational databases—capabilities that are com-
monplace in higher-education student services. This gap underscores the need for com-
plementary mechanisms or integrated architectures that extend RAG to structured data
access and real-time querying.
  To address secure access to structured, student-specific data, the Text-to-SQL para-
digm has emerged as a central research focus. The rise of LLMs has profoundly re-
shaped this field, shifting from specialized, task-specific training toward leveraging the
powerful in-context learning capabilities of general-purpose LLMs [5]. Contemporary
approaches predominantly rely on prompt engineering: the database schema and a small
set of question–SQL exemplars are provided to guide the model in producing executa-
ble queries. Recent work has advanced this line further with techniques such as decom-
posing complex questions and self-repair to improve robustness on difficult tasks [9].
Despite these gains, current methods operate within a clear boundary: inference is con-
strained to information encoded in the database schema. As a result, they remain effec-
tively blind to contextual factors and business rules captured in external, unstructured
sources—such as policy documents—that are often critical for correct interpretation
and compliant query formulation.
  Recognizing the inherent limitations of standalone systems, prior work has explored
hybrid architectures that combine querying over both structured and unstructured data
sources [10]. Early attempts, however, typically relied on rule-based routers or rigid
classifiers to dispatch questions, offering insufficient flexibility to handle composition-
ally complex queries that require multi-step interactions across heterogeneous sources.
The recent rise of AI-agent paradigms, catalyzed by LLMs, has enabled a stronger ap-
proach in which the LLM functions not merely as a text generator but as a reasoning
“controller” capable of planning and autonomously invoking tools [11]. Foundational
frameworks such as ReAct demonstrate that combining reasoning and acting allows
agents to decompose complex problems into sub-tasks and execute them sequentially
in a principled manner [6]. Nevertheless, a gap remains in the design, implementation,

and evaluation of an agent that can flexibly orchestrate between RAG and Text-to-SQL
to address hybrid, structure-rich queries in higher education. Our work directly ad-
dresses this gap by proposing a complete agent architecture that not only selects appro-

hensive answer, thereby offering a practical and novel solution to a high-impact prob-

3    Methodology


3.1   The AI Agent architecture





                          Fig. 1. The AI Agent architecture

   In this section, we present Academic Query Orchestration (AQO), a novel AI-agent
framework designed to address complex information-seeking tasks in higher-education
settings. AQO’s core design objective is to instantiate an autonomous agent capable of
accurately interpreting diverse student queries, devising a structured plan of action, and
flexibly coordinating specialized tools. In doing so, the system retrieves and synthesizes
evidence seamlessly from both unstructured knowledge sources (e.g., policy docu-
ments) and structured databases (e.g., individualized academic records). As illustrated
in Fig. 1, the architecture is modular and organized into three processing stages that
emulate an intelligent perception–cognition–action cycle:
     1.  Perception. A Query Analyst ingests the raw user question, performs prepro-
         cessing and intent understanding, and decomposes composite requests into ac-
         tionable sub-tasks.
     2.  Brain. The agent’s reasoning and planning core, the Orchestrator, consumes
         the analyzed inputs and produces a structured Action Plan that specifies a se-
        quence of steps, the tool to invoke at each step, and the corresponding inputs
         required to resolve the query.
===== PAGE 5 =====
                       Academic Query Orchestration: An AI Agent Framework      5


     3.  Action. The plan is executed by a toolbox of specialized components, includ-
        ing a Knowledge Retriever for unstructured corpora and an SQL Executor for
         structured data access. A Synthesizer then aggregates intermediate results into
        a single, coherent, and well-formed response.
  To enhance robustness, AQO incorporates a feedback loop for self-correction: when
errors or evidence gaps are detected, the agent can revise the plan, adjust tool calls, and
re-synthesize the final answer. The following subsections provide implementation de-
tails and design rationales for each architectural component.


3.2   The Agent's Core: Meta-Prompt Design and Construction

  At the core of Academic Query Orchestration (AQO) lies a unified Brain, responsi-
ble for the end-to-end reasoning pipeline—from perception through planning to ac-
tion—within a single, coherent process. Rather than composing disparate software
modules, AQO leverages the multitask reasoning capabilities of a Large Language
Model (LLM) as the orchestration nucleus. This process is governed by a Meta-Prompt,
a carefully engineered specification that (i) standardizes query analysis (intent parsing
and decomposition), (ii) enforces safety and compliance constraints, and (iii) automat-
ically synthesizes an executable Action Plan that specifies step ordering, tool selection,
and required inputs. This “unified-in-one” design reduces integration complexity, mit-
igates interface mismatches between components, and enhances the overall consistency
and auditability of the reasoning–action pipeline.

Its design comprises a set of strategic components—each specifying objectives, opera-

4    Experiments


4.1   Evaluation Benchmarks


WikiEval Dataset
  To assess a pivotal aspect of our RAG module—its ability to generate factually faith-
ful responses—we employ the WikiEval benchmark, a dataset recognized and inte-
grated into advanced RAG evaluation frameworks such as RAGAS [12]. Unlike tradi-
tional QA datasets, WikiEval is formulated as a verification task: each instance com-
prises (i) a source passage and (ii) a generated claim, with a label indicating the rela-
tionship between them—Supported, Contradicted, or Not Enough Information (NEI).
This is well aligned with our study because it enables direct measurement of the sys-
tem’s capacity to produce answers that are not only relevant but also strictly grounded
in retrieved context from the Unstructured Knowledge Corpus. In our setup, the text
chunks returned by the Knowledge Retriever serve as the source passages, while the
system’s RAG-only answer is treated as the claim to be verified, allowing us to quantify
factual faithfulness and systematically mitigate hallucination risk. Complementing this
verification, we use RAGAS to characterize the end-to-end pipeline with four key met-
rics: Faithfulness (the degree to which the answer adheres to the retrieved context, cap-
turing hallucinations), Answer Relevancy (how tightly the answer remains focused on
the original question), Context Precision (whether the retrieved passages are truly nec-
essary to support the answer, penalizing over-retrieval), and Context Recall (the re-
triever’s ability to surface all evidence required, penalizing under-coverage). Together,
WikiEval and these RAGAS metrics provide convergent evidence on factual ground-
ing, answer focus, and retrieval quality.


HotpotQA Dataset
  To evaluate the framework’s capacity for deep reasoning and handling complex in-
formation flows, we conduct experiments on HotpotQA [14], a multi-hop question-
answering benchmark deliberately constructed so that each question cannot be an-
swered from a single passage. Instead, a system must locate and connect dispersed
“pieces” of evidence across multiple documents to infer the final answer. This design
provides a stringent test of the Agent’s planning and coordination abilities: it probes
not only the retrieval effectiveness of the Knowledge Retriever, but also the Orchestra-
tor’s ability to determine when additional evidence is required and the Synthesizer’s
ability to integrate supporting facts coherently. System performance  is assessed

comprehensively using the benchmark’s four official metrics: (i) Exact Match (EM),
the percentage of predictions that exactly match the gold answer at the character level;
(ii) F1, a more permissive word-level overlap measure capturing agreement for longer
answers; (iii) Precision, the fraction of words in the prediction that also appear in the
gold answer; and (iv) Recall, the fraction of words in the gold answer that are recovered
by the prediction. Collectively, these metrics enable a holistic appraisal of accuracy,
coverage, and reasoning consistency across the end-to-end pipeline.


Spider Dataset
  To rigorously evaluate the capability of the SQL Executor, we adopt the Spider
benchmark [13], widely regarded as the gold standard for complex Text-to-SQL tasks.
Spider comprises thousands of questions spanning hundreds of cross-domain databases
that the model has not previously seen, compelling the system to generate high-com-
plexity SQL, typically involving multi-table JOINs, GROUP BY, ORDER BY, and
nested queries, thereby closely mirroring real-world requirements. This setup enables
quantitative assessment of two core competencies of the underlying LLM (Gemini): (i)
accurately interpreting previously unseen relational schemas, and (ii) faithfully trans-
lating natural-language questions into SQL statements that are correct in both syntax
and semantics. Performance is measured using Spider’s two official, stringent metrics:
Execution Accuracy (EX)—which compares the execution result of the generated query
with that of the ground-truth query—and Exact Match (EM)—which requires the gen-
erated SQL to match the reference query verbatim.


4.2   Experiment Results

  To assess the impact of the underlying large language model (LLM) on the frame-
work’s overall performance, we conducted a head-to-head evaluation of two state-of-
the-art models from Google: Gemini 2.5 Pro, the flagship model with strong complex
reasoning capabilities, and Gemini 2.5 Flash, a lighter variant optimized for speed and
cost efficiency. Both models were deployed within the same system architecture and
operated under an identical Superstructure Prompt to ensure a fair comparison. We rec-
orded and analyzed accuracy and latency metrics to quantitatively characterize differ-
ences in reasoning effectiveness and real-time performance between the two configu-
rations.

5    Practical Examples

  We analyze the information system of Ton Duc Thang University (TDTU) as a rep-
resentative organizational setting to illustrate data use in practice. The system is gov-
erned by a tight integration of two principal data modalities. Structured data—managed
in relational database systems—supports routine operations by maintaining clearly
identified and well-organized records, including student demographics and contact de-
tails, course and curriculum metadata, timetables, and longitudinal aca-demic outcomes
(e.g., grades and accumulated credits). This layer enables reliable querying and auto-
mation of core workflows such as course registration, academic-probation checks, and
GPA computation.

   In parallel, unstructured data—preserved as institutional documents and communi-
cations (e.g., PDFs, word-processing files, policy notices)—encodes the normative and
procedural framework for decision-making, encompassing academic and exami-nation
regulations, student-affairs policies, tuition and scholarship rules, and de-tailed admin-
istrative guidelines. Operational coherence emerges from the interplay between these
layers: rules articulated in documents are operationalized as logical constraints that
guide queries over structured repositories, thereby ensuring con-sistency, compliance,
and auditability. Fig. 3 illustrates this dynamic with three representative scenarios, each

6    Conclusion

   In this work, we introduced Academic Query Orchestration, a novel AI-agent frame-
work for addressing complex hybrid question answering in higher-education settings.
Powered by a Super-Structured Prompt, the agent’s coordinating “brain” autonomously
decomposes user queries, orchestrates knowledge retrieval (RAG) and safe SQL exe-
cution, and synthesizes the resulting evidence into coherent responses. Empirical results
on public benchmarks validate the effectiveness of this integrated approach and demon-
strate advantages over single-capability systems.
  While overall performance depends on the underlying LLM, our findings establish
a viable path toward more capable academic assistants for students. Future directions
include (i) performance optimization across retrieval and execution stages, (ii) expand-
ing the Specialized Tool Suite to cover additional modalities and services, and (iii) en-
dowing the agent with proactive abilities (e.g., anticipation, clarification, and initiative)
under appropriate safety and governance constraints.
